## How it works

The desk lamp sets brightness by position. Closed, it's a plain aluminum cylinder with the light hidden inside. Turn it up and a lantern of two frosted glass tubes around a lit core rises out of the top, up to about 115 mm. Every brightness change is a mechanical move, so the motor, the light and the smart-home state all have to agree on where the lantern is.

The light fades in at the start of a rise and out at the end of a descent, so motion speed is also fade speed. The core carries a warm and cool LED strip wound around it as a helix, with a row of addressable LEDs at the top.

## Mechanism

A brushless gimbal motor turns a lead screw directly, and quiet came first in every choice. The lantern rides a floating anti-backlash nut and two steel rods with polymer bushings, so a slightly off-center screw can't bind it. The screw is self-locking, so at rest the motor driver sleeps and there's no hum.

The tube is the only metal part of the body. Everything else is printed in ABS, and the outside shows just three thin lines of plastic: a lip under the tube, a collar on top and the rim of the lid. No moving printed part touches a fixed one; where parts meet, the contact is steel, POM or felt, and every screw into plastic goes into a heat-set insert. I checked clearances over the whole travel with a scripted sweep of the Fusion assembly.

## Control and firmware

A magnetic encoder on the motor closes the loop, so the firmware knows where the lantern is instead of counting steps. If someone stops it by hand, the motor gives up after about half a second, takes that spot as the new target and reports the matching level over Matter. Over Matter the lamp is a color temperature light, and its brightness slider drives the motor position. A knob under the base covers local control.

The firmware is ESP-IDF with SimpleFOC, on an ESP32-S3 control board I designed. The field-oriented control loop gets one of the chip's two cores to itself, so radio traffic doesn't stall the motor.
